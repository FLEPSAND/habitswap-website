You were doing well. Maybe it was 10 days, maybe 90. Then last night happened, and now there's a knot in your stomach and a voice in your head saying you've blown it again. If you searched for what to do after a relapse, you're in the right place, and you're far from the only one.

Here's the short answer. One slip is not the same as a relapse, and what turns one into the other is mostly what you think and do in the hours afterward. Stop where you are, take care of your body, tell one person, look honestly at what led up to it, and change one thing in your plan. The next choice matters more than the last one.

## Is it a slip or a relapse?

Addiction counselors often separate two things. A **lapse**, or slip, is a single event: one drink, one night of porn, one bet. A **relapse** is going back to the old pattern, when one time turns into days and weeks.

Psychologist Alan Marlatt built his relapse prevention model around this difference (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985, *Relapse Prevention*). The useful part is simple. There is ground between a lapse and a full relapse, and you have a say in what happens on that ground.

Maybe you're past the single slip already and have been back in the old routine for a while. Everything below still applies. The way back starts in the same place: the next decision.

## The "I've already blown it" trap

You probably know this thought: "Well, the streak is gone anyway. Might as well keep going today." Marlatt called this the **abstinence violation effect**. When you've set a strict rule and break it once, it's tempting to throw the whole rule out.

Two things usually feed it. One is shame: "I'm just weak." The other is all-or-nothing thinking, where you're either perfectly clean or completely lost. Witkiewitz and Marlatt describe how this kind of thinking can turn a single lapse into a longer relapse (Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2004, *American Psychologist*).

Picture Mike. He hasn't had a drink in five weeks. At a work party on Thursday, he has two beers. On Friday he thinks, "I already broke it, so what's the point," and buys a six-pack on the way home. Thursday was the slip. Friday came from a thought.

The antidote is a sentence you prepare before you need it. Something like: "That was a slip. The next choice is the one that counts." Put it in your phone notes so it's there when the "what's the point" voice shows up.

## What to do in the first 24 hours after a relapse

The hours right after a slip are fragile. The shame is fresh, the habit has been switched back on, and it's often late and you're tired. What helps now is pretty ordinary.

**The first hour.** Stop where you are. No "just for tonight." Close the tab, pour the rest down the sink, put the phone in another room. Take a few slow breaths and stand up. Drink a glass of water or step outside for a minute. Let your body know something different is happening now.

**That night.** If it's late, go to bed. Thinking through your failures at midnight rarely leads anywhere good. If the urge comes back, use your swap or your emergency plan, the same as any other day.

**The next morning.** Eat a real breakfast. Do one small, normal thing that's good for you: a walk, a workout, a prayer, getting to work on time. The day after a slip is a regular day, not a day of punishment.

**Within 24 hours.** Tell someone you trust. A friend, your spouse, a sponsor, someone from church or your recovery group. A text is enough: "Had a slip last night. Feeling rough, but I'm getting back on track." Secrets get heavier the longer you carry them. Saying it out loud takes some of the weight off.

What to skip during these 24 hours: punishing yourself, making panicked vows you can't keep tomorrow, hiding it, or telling yourself it wasn't a big deal. Beating yourself up and brushing it off both keep you from looking at what actually happened.

## Look back honestly, without beating yourself up

Once the first wave has passed, take a calm look back. Not an interrogation. More like the way you'd help a friend who told you the same story. A few notes are enough:

- When and where did it happen?
- How were you feeling beforehand? Tired, stressed, lonely, bored, angry?
- What happened right before?
- Where was the last point you could have turned around?

A simple check from recovery circles is HALT: were you hungry, angry, lonely, or tired? One of those is very often in the mix.

That last question usually shows you the slip didn't come out of nowhere. There was a chain: a long day, an empty apartment, phone in bed, "just a quick look." Every link in that chain is a place you can step in next time.

The earlier in the chain you decide, the easier it is. Job "made a covenant with my eyes" (Job 31:1). He didn't wait until temptation was strong. He decided before the first look. For you, that might mean the phone never comes to bed, and "just a quick look" is off the table.

Being kind to yourself here isn't letting yourself off the hook. In a study by Breines and Chen (2012, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*), people who treated a mistake with self-compassion were more motivated to improve than people who were hard on themselves. You can take full responsibility without tearing yourself down.

## Adjust your plan

A slip shows you where your plan had a gap. It's tempting to overhaul everything at once. One concrete change that fits the trigger works better.

One exception: if something keeps pulling you back into the same sin, the change can and should be drastic. Jesus said it's better to cut off what makes you stumble (Matthew 5:29–30). Deleting the app, canceling the subscription, pouring out the bottle isn't overreacting. It's taking the fight seriously.

If-then plans are a good tool here. Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer studied these "implementation intentions": you decide in advance what you'll do in a specific situation. A few examples:

| What happened | A possible fix |
|---|---|
| Alone in bed with your phone late at night | If it's 10 p.m., my phone charges in the kitchen. |
| Drank after an argument | If I'm wound up after a fight, I go for a 10-minute walk. |
| Bored on a Sunday afternoon | If Sunday is empty, I make plans with someone on Saturday. |
| Your swap wasn't within reach | My running shoes now live by the front door. |

Also check whether your replacement fits the trigger. If loneliness sends you to your phone, push-ups won't do much, but texting a friend might. For more on picking a replacement that works, see [how to break a bad habit](/en/guides/how-to-break-a-bad-habit/).

If you're dealing with porn, filters, Screen Time settings, and an accountability partner often matter even more. The full plan is in [how to quit porn](/en/guides/how-to-quit-porn/).

## Grace and starting over

If you're a Christian, a slip can feel like more than a broken plan. When it's something the Bible calls sin, it is that. You didn't just miss a goal. You went against God. Then comes the idea that you need to feel bad for a while before you're allowed to pray again.

The Bible doesn't work that way. God isn't waiting for you to punish yourself long enough:

> "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous to forgive us the sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." (1 John 1:9, WEB)

Confessing means telling God honestly what happened and calling it sin where it was sin. No excuses, no long self-trial. Then you get to accept forgiveness instead of trying to earn it. Guilt that never ends doesn't make you freer. It makes you tired.

In the parable of the lost son, the father runs to meet his son while he is "still far off" (Luke 15:20). The son hasn't even finished his speech yet. That's how Jesus describes God's response to someone who turns back.

The prophet Micah wrote something that fits the morning after a slip:

> "When I fall, I will arise. When I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me." (Micah 7:8, WEB)

Grace isn't permission to stay down. It's what gets you back up. Proverbs 24:16 says that "a righteous man falls seven times and rises up again." It doesn't say he never falls.

And another verse for the next morning: God's mercies "are new every morning. Great is your faithfulness." (Lamentations 3:22–23, WEB)

If you don't share this faith, this part is still for you. The forgiveness John writes about is for anyone who turns to God, not just people who've believed for years. You don't have to punish yourself first.

## When to get help

Sometimes adjusting your plan isn't enough. Reach out for support if:

- slips are piling up, or single slips have turned back into a daily pattern
- you keep losing control even when you're really trying
- your relationships, work, health, or money are taking a hit
- you feel hopeless or down for days after a slip

Good places to start are your doctor (in the UK, your GP), a licensed counselor or therapist, or a local addiction service. In the US, the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 is free, confidential, and open 24/7, and can point you to treatment and support groups near you. If you have faith, a pastor can walk with you too. One doesn't replace the other.

One important note about alcohol and sedatives like benzodiazepines: if you've been using a lot for a long time, stopping suddenly can be physically dangerous. Talk to a doctor before you stop again.

If you're thinking about suicide or you're in danger, don't wait. The numbers are right below this article.

## Your next step today

You don't have to sort everything out today. Do two things. Text one person you trust and tell them what happened. Then write down your sentence for next time: "That was a slip. The next choice is the one that counts."

Yesterday happened. So did every good day before it, and those aren't gone.
