It's 11:40 p.m. You told yourself you'd put the phone down at 11. The news is bad, the videos are mostly forgettable, and your eyes hurt, but your thumb keeps going. When you finally stop, you don't feel rested. You feel wired and a little hollow.

If that sounds familiar, you're not lazy and you're not broken. You're up against products designed to keep you watching. This guide explains how to stop doomscrolling in a way that actually sticks: what's going on, how to take an honest look at your own habits, and the specific changes that make the biggest difference.

**The short answer:** To stop doomscrolling, change your setup instead of relying on willpower. Turn off most notifications, move the worst apps off your home screen, set app limits in Screen Time, and keep your phone out of the bedroom at night. Then decide in advance what you'll do in the bored moments when you'd normally reach for it.

## Why is doomscrolling so hard to stop?

Your phone is a useful tool. The trouble starts with apps whose business depends on how long you stay. Two design features do most of the work.

The first is **unpredictable rewards**. Sometimes there's a message, a funny clip, or a post you really care about. Often there's nothing. Because you never know when the good stuff will show up, you keep checking. Rewards that come only some of the time are known in behavioral psychology to build especially sticky habits. Your brain responds to the anticipation as much as to what you actually find.

The second is **infinite scroll**. A newspaper had a last page. A feed doesn't. There's no natural stopping point, so every time you stop, you have to make an active decision. That decision is hardest late at night, when you're tired and alone.

Doomscrolling adds a third layer. Bad news grabs attention because it feels urgent, like you need to know. But reading the tenth take on the same crisis rarely helps you act. It mostly leaves you anxious and still scrolling.

None of this means all phone use is harmful. A FaceTime call with your grandmother is not the same as two hours of short videos in bed. What matters is what you're doing and whether you chose it.

## Is it phone addiction or just a bad habit?

"Phone addiction" is an everyday phrase, not a formal diagnosis. For most people, "a habit that isn't good for me" is closer to the truth. That doesn't make it trivial, but it's good news: habits can change. Our guide on [how to break a bad habit](/en/guides/how-to-break-a-bad-habit/) covers the basics.

It's more serious when your phone use is clearly hurting your sleep, work, school, or relationships, and you haven't been able to cut back despite real effort. In that case, talk to your doctor or a licensed counselor. If your scrolling is tied to anxiety or low mood, that's worth bringing up too, because the phone is often a way of coping with something else.

## An honest self-check

Start with the numbers. On an iPhone, open Settings, then Screen Time. You'll see your daily average, which apps take the most time, and how often you pick the phone up. It can be uncomfortable to look at. It's still a better starting point than a guess.

Then answer a few questions honestly:

- Do I check my phone before I'm fully awake?
- Do I pick it up without knowing what I'm looking for?
- Do I go to bed later than I meant to because I'm still scrolling?
- Do I feel uneasy when the phone isn't within reach?
- After a long scroll, do I usually feel worse than before?
- Have I tried to cut back more than once and slid back?

A few yeses aren't a verdict on your character. They're a map. Notice when it happens, too. For most people it's the same handful of moments: waiting in line, boredom, avoiding a hard task, alone in the evening, right after an argument. The HALT check from recovery circles helps here: am I hungry, angry, lonely, or tired? We go deeper in [habit triggers and the HALT method](/en/guides/habit-triggers-halt/).

## How to stop doomscrolling: practical steps

Habit research keeps pointing in the same direction: your environment matters more than your resolve. Psychologist Wendy Wood has written about how people who seem to have great self-control often just have fewer temptations in their way. For your phone, that means adding small speed bumps where you grab it on autopilot.

### Set limits in Screen Time

Go to Settings → Screen Time. Use App Limits to cap a single app or a whole category, and use Downtime to set hours when only the apps you choose are available. Depending on your iOS version, these may be called Time Allowances and Screen Time Schedule instead.

Don't start at zero. If you spend two hours a day in one app, a one-hour limit is a solid first step. If you know you'll just tap "Ignore Limit," ask someone you trust to set the Screen Time passcode.

### Cut your notifications

Go through your apps one by one and ask: does this app get to interrupt me? Messages from people you love, yes. Your calendar, maybe. Likes, suggestions, and "you might have missed this," no. Almost every app works just as well when you open it on your own terms.

### Clean up your home screen

Move the apps that pull you in hardest off the first screen and into a folder on the last page. It sounds too simple to matter, but much of your phone use isn't a decision at all. It's a reflex. A couple of extra swipes can be enough to make you notice what you're doing. Keep the first screen for tools you use on purpose: maps, camera, calendar, maybe a Bible app.

### Keep your phone out of reach at night

Charge it in another room, or at least across the room so you'd have to get out of bed. A cheap alarm clock solves the wake-up problem. Late-night scrolling eats into sleep, and being short on sleep makes you more likely to repeat the pattern the next day.

### Protect the first hour of your day

If the first thing you take in each morning is news, email, and feeds, other people set the tone for your day. Try a simple rule: the phone stays down for the first hour after you get up. If an hour feels impossible, start with 30 minutes. Get dressed, make coffee, open a window, pray, think about what matters today. Then pick up the phone, as someone who has already arrived.

### Have a replacement for the empty moments

This is the step most people skip. When you only take something away, you leave a gap, and the old habit fills it fast. So decide ahead of time what you'll do instead. Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer's research on if-then plans (he calls them implementation intentions) shows that spelling out the situation and the response makes follow-through more likely:

- If I'm waiting for the bus, then I'll look around or read the paperback in my bag.
- If I reach for my phone on the couch at night, then I'll call a friend or take a ten-minute walk.
- If I'm putting off a hard task, then I'll set a five-minute timer and just start.

Boredom isn't the enemy. A few minutes without input is rest for your mind, not wasted time. In Habit Swap you can write this kind of plan as your swap plan for your usual trigger moments, and the emergency coach is there when an urge hits hard.

## What if I still lose an evening to my phone?

You will, at some point. One bad night doesn't erase your progress. The next day, take a quick look: when did it start, what was going on, what would have helped? Change one thing and keep going. Our guide on [what to do after a relapse](/en/guides/relapse-what-to-do/) walks through this.

Give it time, too. In a 2010 study in the European Journal of Social Psychology, Phillippa Lally and colleagues found it took a median of 66 days for a new behavior to become automatic, with a wide range from person to person. More on that in [how long it takes to break a habit](/en/guides/how-long-does-it-take-to-break-a-habit/).

## Silence and prayer instead of constant input

Constant input has a side effect you only notice once it's gone: there's no room left to think, grieve, be grateful, or pray. Many people find that a few days with less scrolling bring up thoughts they had been swiping away. That can feel uncomfortable at first. Often, though, it's exactly the space where something can start to heal.

Paul wrote something to the church in Corinth that fits our phones surprisingly well:

> "‘All things are lawful for me,’ but not all things are expedient. ‘All things are lawful for me,’ but I will not be brought under the power of anything." (1 Corinthians 6:12, WEB)

Your phone isn't forbidden. The question is whether you're using it or it's holding you. Elsewhere Paul writes:

> "Therefore watch carefully how you walk, not as unwise, but as wise, redeeming the time, because the days are evil." (Ephesians 5:15–16, WEB)

Redeeming the time doesn't have to become another performance. It can start small: take the five minutes you'd normally spend opening a feed and be quiet instead. Tell God what's on your mind, or just sit with him. Jesus invites tired people specifically: "Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest." (Matthew 11:28, WEB) That rest is a gift, not something you earn by scrolling less.

If faith isn't your thing, the core still holds. Silence isn't empty. A walk without earbuds or a dinner with the phone in another room gives you something no feed can.

## One thing you can do tonight

Take ten minutes before bed. Open Screen Time, find the app that eats the most of your day, turn off its notifications, and move it off your home screen. Then pick the spot where your phone will charge tonight, somewhere other than next to your pillow. That's enough for today.
